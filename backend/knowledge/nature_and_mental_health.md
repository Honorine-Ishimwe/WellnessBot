# Nature Exposure and Mental Health

**Category:** Mindfulness
**Source:** Environmental Health Perspectives, Stanford University Research

## The Science of Nature and Well-Being

Research consistently demonstrates that spending time in nature reduces cortisol levels, lowers blood pressure, decreases heart rate, reduces anxiety and rumination, improves mood, and enhances cognitive function.

## Forest Bathing (Shinrin-yoku)

Originated in Japan in the 1980s, forest bathing involves slowly and mindfully walking through a forested area while engaging all five senses. Studies show that a 2-hour forest bathing session increases natural killer cell activity (immune function) for up to 30 days.

## The 120-Minute Threshold

A major study published in Scientific Reports found that spending at least 120 minutes per week in nature (all at once or accumulated) is associated with significantly better health and well-being. Below 120 minutes, the benefits were minimal.

## Ways to Get More Nature Exposure

- Walk in a park during lunch breaks
- Garden (even container gardening on a balcony counts)
- Eat meals outside when weather permits
- Exercise outdoors instead of in a gym when possible
- Take phone calls while walking outside
- Weekend hikes or nature trail walks
- Sit near a window with a view of trees or sky
- Bring nature indoors with houseplants

## Urban Nature Counts

You do not need to visit a remote wilderness. Urban parks, tree-lined streets, community gardens, and even indoor plants provide measurable benefits. The key is mindful engagement with natural elements rather than the location itself.

## Blue Spaces

Proximity to water (oceans, lakes, rivers, streams) provides additional mental health benefits. Even listening to water sounds or looking at images of water scenes has calming effects.
